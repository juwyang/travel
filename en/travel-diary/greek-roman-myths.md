Caravaggio, Bacchus

Bacchus, the Roman god of wine; Dionysus in Greek mythology

Cardinal del Monte — Caravaggio's patron, a man of artistic taste

Greek and Roman gods mostly have counterparts:

Greek Zeus, Roman Jupiter. Classic story: turning into a bull to carry off the Phoenician princess Europa; thunderbolts

God of wine: Dionysus // Bacchus. Invented wine and travelled the world with his followers, spreading the art of winemaking

Goddess of love and beauty: Aphrodite // Venus. The golden apple, the Trojan War

Sun god: Apollo. Struck by Cupid's arrow — Apollo and Daphne, Bernini's sculpture in the Galleria Borghese

Queen of the gods: Hera // Juno. Spent her life matching wits with Zeus's lovers. When Zeus went out, he sent Echo, a mountain nymph, to keep Hera busy with gossip. When Hera realised, she cursed Echo so that she could only repeat what others said.

The sea: Poseidon // Neptune. Zeus, Poseidon and Hades are three brothers; he is Zeus's elder brother. Atlantis

God of the underworld: Hades // Pluto

God of fire and the forge: Hephaestus // Vulcan

Goddess of wisdom: Athena // Minerva. How Athens got its name: Athena gave the citizens the olive tree, and Poseidon gave them a spring — but it was salty seawater.

War: Ares // Mars. Caught by the fire god having an affair with his wife Aphrodite, goddess of beauty; much embarrassment

Goddess of the hunt: Artemis. Apollo's sister; shot and killed her beloved Orion (the constellation)

The Sirens; the great hero Odysseus; the Trojan War. It began with the myth of the golden apple: Aphrodite helped the Trojan prince carry off the beautiful Helen, Queen of Sparta, leading Sparta to attack Troy (in Turkey). The Greeks besieged Troy for ten years; finally Odysseus proposed the wooden horse. The Greeks feigned a retreat, the Trojans dragged the horse into the city as a trophy, the Greeks hidden inside burst out, and Troy was put to the sword. "Odyssey" means the story of Odysseus. On the way home, passing the Strait of Messina, he had his sailors stop their ears with beeswax and had himself tied to the ship, and so got past the Sirens' song.

Medusa. Originally a beautiful priestess in Athena's temple, she was violated by Poseidon at Athena's altar; Athena then punished her by turning her hair into venomous snakes and giving her a gaze that turned people to stone. Later the hero Perseus, using the reflection in Athena's bronze shield so as not to look at her directly, killed Medusa. He then used Medusa's head as a tool to turn the sea monster Cetus to stone, rescuing the Ethiopian princess Andromeda; they later became the constellations Perseus and Andromeda.

Prometheus stole fire and brought it to humankind; Zeus chained him to a mountain where a giant eagle tore at him, until Heracles shot the eagle.

Orpheus and Eurydice. Eurydice was bitten by a venomous snake and fell into the underworld. Orpheus's wondrous music quieted the three-headed hound of hell, made the tormented stop groaning, and brought Hades to tears. Hades made an exception and let Orpheus take his wife back to the world of the living, the two walking one behind the other — but he must not look back on the way. When he saw the sunlight, Orpheus turned around, but Eurydice's feet were still in the shadow, and her body dissolved into a wisp of smoke.

Heracles, the Greek demigod of strength, famous for his Twelve Labours. The second labour was slaying the Hydra. In the swamp of Lerna, every head cut off the Hydra grew back as two; Heracles called his nephew, and as he cut off each head, his nephew seared the wound with a torch.\
He was the son of Zeus and a mortal woman. Cursed by Hera, he went mad and killed his own wife. He spent the rest of his life atoning, and after his mortal death was raised to Olympus as a god, where he married Hebe, goddess of youth. He killed the lion, slew the Hydra, caught the hind, captured the boar, cleaned the stables, drove off the monstrous birds, subdued the bull, caught the mares, took the girdle, led away the cattle, picked the golden apples, and captured alive Cerberus, the three-headed hound of hell.

Pandora. To take revenge on Prometheus, Zeus fashioned a perfect woman. Pandora means "the giver of all gifts"; blessed by the gods, she married Prometheus's brother and was given a wedding present, Pandora's box. Because Pandora had also been given curiosity, she opened the box and released sickness, greed, war, suffering and more. She closed it, but shut hope in at the very bottom.

The wings of Icarus. Fly too high and the sun melts them; fly too low and the sea soaks them. A story about getting carried away.

Achilles, the Greek war hero of the Trojan War, a demigod. As a demigod he could either live a long, ordinary life, or win eternal fame and die young. As a baby his mother dipped him in the River Styx, making him invulnerable, but because she held him by his right heel, that spot was his weakness. A poisoned Trojan arrow struck him there, and he took the path of eternal fame and an early death.

Sisyphus, a cunning mortal, informed on Zeus for abducting a river god's daughter, and locked Death, who came for him, in his own dungeon. In the end he was punished by having to push a boulder up a hill in the underworld.

Oedipus. The oracle said he would kill his father and marry his mother. Abandoned as a baby and raised by adoptive parents, he solved the riddle of the Sphinx and won the throne of Thebes.

The Oracle of Delphi is the Temple of Apollo. Apollo shot the ancient serpent Python and built his temple here at Delphi. There was a priestess, whose title was the Pythia, who went into a trance from the vapours rising out of a crack in the rock and spoke in a state of communion.

Nymphs — fairies, spirits. Minor female deities below the major gods.\
Tree nymphs, Dryads: trees and forests\
Water nymphs, Naiads: streams, springs and lakes\
Sea nymphs, Nereids: the sea\
Mountain nymphs, Oreads: wild mountains, deep valleys and rocks.

The lyre — the ancient Greek seven-stringed instrument

History

The most brilliant classical period of ancient Greek philosophy:

Socrates, 470–399 BC. Plato's teacher. "Know thyself"

Plato, 427–347 BC. The allegory of the cave; The Republic; utopia; the Academy in Athens

Aristotle, 384–322 BC. An encyclopaedic scholar; logic and the syllogism; tutor to Alexander the Great

Ancient Greek culture:

\[Democracy\], \[Literature and drama\]: Homer's epics, the Iliad and the Odyssey; drama. \[Science and mathematics\]: Pythagoras, Euclid, Archimedes, Hippocrates. \[Art and architecture\]: perfect proportions, the muscular ideal — the Venus de Milo, the Discobolus; the Doric, Ionic and Corinthian orders of the Parthenon. \[Spirit\]: an emphasis on the human (the body, desire), and on reason
